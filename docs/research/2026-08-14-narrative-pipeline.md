# Narrative pipeline — how scripts for games and film are actually produced

Research record for wayfinder map #156, story workstream (asked by James on
[Story design #175](https://github.com/fol2/glassvow/issues/175)'s heels:
"how do they normally do scripts for games/movies?"). Produced 2026-08-14 by a
5-sweep research workflow (film/TV rooms · game-studio pipelines · Hades
specifically · systemic-delivery literature · twist craft), 82 references and
47 transferable practices, all web-verified this session — anything an agent
could not confirm is tagged **[unverified]** inline, per the project's
evidence standard.

## The convergent shape

Across film, TV, and games, every mature pipeline is the same ladder, and it
is never "write 100K words then see":

1. **Lock the destination first.** Twist writers are unanimous: Coben ("I know
   the ending before I even start writing"), Only Murders' room ("work
   backwards... creating the true crime, then piecing it together"), Shyamalan
   (ending in mind "so that in every episode you can write traces of it").
   Severance keeps one canonical lore doc that answers mysteries before they
   air. The Sixth Sense's twist was then *retrofitted* across ~five further
   full drafts — the quality lives in the retrofit pass, not the first draft.
2. **Break story on a board before anyone writes pages.** The writers' room
   spends days on beats (board → outline → pages; Grillo-Marxuach's Eleven
   Laws); McKee's step-outline method spends most of the schedule pre-pages;
   Save the Cat's board does the same solo. Pages written before the break is
   settled get thrown away.
3. **A bible plus a continuity enforcer.** The show bible carries canon; the
   script coordinator diffs every draft and hunts contradictions as a *job*,
   not a hope. Dark's writers re-audited every plant on every draft ("map it
   out again, write new Post-Its"); Pixar's braintrust reviews name problems,
   not solutions.
4. **Games split narrative design from writing.** Narrative design decides how
   the story reaches the player (channels, triggers, gating); writing produces
   lines. Systemic copy is a **database, not a script**: storylets =
   {preconditions, content, effects} (Kreminski/Wardrip-Fruin), picked by a
   salience system where the most specific eligible line wins with generic
   fallbacks (Valve's Response System), with once-only flags, cooldowns, and
   priority tiers (Hades' three tiers: evergreen / condition-triggered /
   essential-overrides).
5. **Hades specifically** — our rubric anchor — is one writer authoring
   directly into the game's data format: 300K words, 21K+ lines, lines keyed
   to durable state flags (never to line order), loss beats the richest pool
   ("the moment of death isn't about rage-quitting"), hyper-specific
   reactions bought disproportionate perceived depth (Hypnos' 75
   cause-of-death lines), and the endings written **last**, held out of Early
   Access entirely.

## Film / TV — the writers’ room ladder

### References

- [The Eleven Laws of Showrunning](https://okbjgm.weebly.com/uploads/3/1/5/0/31506003/11_laws_of_showrunning_nice_version.pdf) — Javier Grillo-Marxuach, 2016 (article)
  Primary-source essay by the Lost/The Witcher writer-producer (full PDF downloaded and text-extracted this session). Documents the board→outline→script ladder: the showrunner must 'see the gross anatomy of the stories the writers pitch you off the shorthand of the board', then the 'slightly more detailed treatment of the story, plot, and scenes in an outline', then notes/rewrites on the script. States the industry norm that 'a week to a week and a half is considered ample time to write the first draft of a script from a solid story break and outline', that repeatedly returning outlined/scripted stories to the board means the break process is broken, and that at any moment six stories are in flight: on the board, in outline, in script, in prep, in production, in post. Also: the showrunner must articulate 'a strong, clear vision' — 'draw the boundaries of the sandbox with precision, detail, consistency, and integrity' — and preach it constantly.
- [What's the Rewrite Process Like in a TV Writers Room?](https://michaeljamin.substack.com/p/whats-the-rewrite-process-like-in) — Michael Jamin (sitcom writer/producer), 2023 (article)
  Working TV writer's account of the episode pipeline: the staff spends 3–5 days in the room breaking 'all the scenes and act breaks'; one writer then produces outline, first draft, second draft with showrunner notes; then 'the writers room (working as a whole) will re-write the script, creating a "Table Draft"'; actors read the Table Draft at the table read; 'after the table read, the script will be rewritten some more.' Notes the failure detector: 'it's easy to tell when something isn't working, because the energy will leave the room' — the staff pitches fixes until the showrunner approves. (Tail of the article is paywalled.)
- [Writers' Room 101: Your First Writers' Room](https://scriptmag.com/features/writers-room-101-first-writers-room) — Eric Haywood, 2014 (article)
  TV drama writer's column on room mechanics: writers around a conference table take turns pitching; a writers' assistant documents everything said; someone stands at the dry-erase boards writing out the beats so the whole room stays aligned; 'at the end of the day, the writers' assistant will e-mail everyone a document containing the day's room notes' and writers review them before the next session; the head writer runs the room when the showrunner is pulled to production; the cycle repeats 'until all stories are broken and all scripts are written.'
- [Creativity, Inc. — 'Inside the Braintrust' (excerpt)](https://pixarpost.com/2014/03/the-pixar-braintrust-excerpt-from-ed.html) — Ed Catmull (with Amy Wallace), 2014 (book)
  Catmull's own text (book excerpt republished at Pixar Post; the Fast Company version returned 403 this session). The Braintrust reviews films in development with two structural rules: it has no authority — directors are not obligated to implement specific notes, but must genuinely address the problems identified — and feedback names problems rather than prescribing solutions, because a director may not see a flaw since 'much of what he thinks is visible on-screen is only visible in his own head.' Candor is the engine: 'early on, all of our movies suck', and the work is moving them 'from suck to not-suck', sometimes requiring you to 'blow something up or start over.'
- [How Pixar Fosters Collective Creativity](https://hbr.org/2008/09/how-pixar-fosters-collective-creativity) — Ed Catmull, 2008 (article) **[unverified — could not confirm in-session]**
  Located and fetched this session, but only the opening was readable (body paywalled): Catmull rejects the idea-hunting view of creativity as 'rooted in a misguided view of creativity that exaggerates the importance of the initial idea' — people and process over the pitch. Could NOT confirm in-session the article's widely cited sections on dailies (showing incomplete work every day) and postmortems; treat those specifics as unconfirmed here.
- [Save the Cat! The Last Book on Screenwriting You'll Ever Need](https://savethecat.com/get-started) — Blake Snyder, 2005 (book)
  Official Save the Cat site (fetched) confirms the 15-beat beat sheet with percentage placements — Opening Image (0–1%) through Catalyst (10%), Midpoint (50%), All Is Lost (75%), Break into Three (80%), Final Image (99–100%). The companion page https://savethecat.com/story-cards (also fetched) confirms the two-tier tooling: 15 Beat Cards ('a solid framework for your plot') then 40 Scene Cards ('transform the general idea into concrete, living scenes'), and Snyder's claim that 'the Board is the most vital piece of equipment a writer needs to have at their disposal.' The four-row act layout of the Board is documented in the book / Wikipedia entry surfaced in search, not on the fetched pages.
- [Story: Substance, Structure, Style and the Principles of Screenwriting](https://www.shortform.com/blog/how-do-you-write-a-story/) — Robert McKee, 1997 (book)
  Via a located secondary (Shortform's summary of the book; the book itself not fetched): McKee's 'write from the inside out' method — spend most of the schedule (roughly four of six months) on a step-outline of 3x5 cards, one stack per act, one or two sentences per scene describing what happens and how it turns; then expand to a treatment (moment-by-moment present-tense prose including what characters think and feel — text and subtext); only then write the screenplay with dialogue. The payoff claimed: the inside-out writer knows within weeks whether a story works; the outside-in writer spends a year clinging to favorite scenes.
- [Script Revision Colors Explained — A Guide for Filmmakers](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-are-script-revision-colors/) — StudioBinder, 2020 (article)
  'The script color order is a standard that was set up by the Writers Guild of America': white production draft, then blue, pink, yellow, green, goldenrod, buff, salmon, cherry, cycling with addendums if revisions continue. Only changed pages (or full scripts) are reissued on the new color; the title page logs every revision's date and color; in digital workflows the revision color and marks still appear as text on the title page and page headers so everyone can tell current from stale.
- [What's a Script Coordinator?](https://scriptation.com/blog/whats-a-script-coordinator/) — Scriptation (industry blog), 2021 (article)
  The script coordinator proofreads every draft, enforces the show's house format ('know what their show's script is supposed to look like, then make it look the same way every time'), and is 'the first line of defense when it comes to not just script, but show continuity' — checking character facts (names, ages, allergies) and plot consistency across episodes, catching duplicate jokes and contradictions. They diff drafts to identify exactly what changed and alert affected department heads, then convert and distribute the current script to the crew, including color-coded revision pages.
- [A Guide to TV Show Bibles](https://scriptation.com/blog/tv-show-bible-and-character-bibles-guide/) — Scriptation (industry blog), 2021 (article)
  The show bible carries the creator's vision — characters, setting, time, tone, world rules — 'everything that needs to be known to someone trying to make the show.' During production a character bible is maintained by support staff (script coordinator, writers' assistant, or writers' PA), logging per-episode facts: relatives, birthdays, medical conditions, significant firsts and lasts ('first kiss, last drink before sobriety') so 'Did we say if Andy had a sister?' has a documented answer. It is 'a living document that can be accessed by everyone', with review passes both before shooting and after final cut.
- [What is a Table Read?](https://nofilmschool.com/What-is-a-table-read) — No Film School, 2021 (article)
  A table read gathers cast, writer(s), and director late in preproduction to read the script aloud before shooting. Its functions: hearing the script performed for the first time exposes dialogue and pacing problems invisible on the page; actors' interpretations reveal character nuances the writers missed; directors spot production issues. Afterward the group 'circle[s] up after to make revisions' — the read is explicitly a gate before production, with rewrites folded in before cameras roll. (Michael Jamin's piece above confirms sitcoms run this gate every single episode.)
- [Writing for Episodic TV: From Freelance to Showrunner](http://leegoldberg.com/writing_for_epi/) — Writers Guild of America, West, 2004 (article) **[unverified — could not confirm in-session]**
  WGAw brochure on the episodic staffing ladder and what each writing level owes the production. I confirmed its existence via two reliable secondaries this session (TV writer Lee Goldberg's site, which says 'The WGA has posted their informative brochure ... online for download as a series of PDF files', and Erik Bork's blog), but the wga.org link they point to is dead and I could not reach the primary PDF — its internal content is NOT verified here.

### Transferable practices

- **Escalating-commitment ladder: beats before pages** — Film and TV never draft cold. McKee: months on a step-outline (1–2 sentences per scene on cards), expand to treatment, only then screenplay — 'the inside-out writer knows within weeks whether a story works.' Snyder: logline, then 15-beat sheet with percentage placements, then ~40 scene cards on the Board, then pages. TV: board → outline → draft, and Grillo-Marxuach says a showrunner who repeatedly bounces outlined/scripted stories back to the board has a broken break process, not a drafting problem.
  *For glassvow*: Before drafting any of the 100K zh chars: one logline for the twist arc, a 15-beat sheet for the whole meta-story, then a card-level break of each bucket (each of the five recurring-character meeting chains is its own mini beat-row; run-start/interstitial/win-loss pools get a 'what this pool is allowed to know at each act' card). The human reviewer signs off at beat level. A reveal problem caught on a card costs minutes; caught after the en rewrite it costs the whole two-language pipeline twice.
- **Break story on the board, together, before assigning pages** — The room pitches; one person stands at the dry-erase board writing the beats so everyone tracks the same story; the writers' assistant records everything and emails room notes nightly; 3–5 days per episode break. Only after the break does one writer take the episode away to outline and draft. The board shorthand is the shared contract the showrunner reads 'gross anatomy' from.
  *For glassvow*: With one AI writer and one human, the board is a doc: per bucket, the AI pitches beat options with alternatives, the human picks, and the approved board doc becomes the contract every draft is diffed against. It also plays the writers'-assistant role — decisions and rejected pitches get recorded in the doc, because a two-person pipeline has no room memory to fall back on.
- **Draft fast from a solid break; the break is the slow part** — Grillo-Marxuach: 'a week to a week and a half is considered ample time to write the first draft of a script from a solid story break and outline.' The room invests days in the break precisely so drafting is quick and rarely page-one-rewritten.
  *For glassvow*: Budget accordingly: beat-contract negotiation with the human is the long pole; AI drafting a bucket from an approved contract should be hours. Diagnostic rule: if the human keeps rejecting drafts at the prose level, stop looping on prose — the beat contract is wrong, go back to the board.
- **Show bible + character ledger as living continuity law** — The bible holds vision, characters, tone, and world rules; during production a character bible logs every canon fact the moment it airs (ages, allergies, firsts and lasts), maintained by a named support role, with review passes before shooting and again after final cut. 'Did we say if Andy had a sister?' must have a documented answer.
  *For glassvow*: Matches the glassvow-story skill's bible discipline: keep bible + foreshadow ledger in-repo, and give every canon fact that ships in a string a ledger row (string ID → fact → which act may know it). For a twist story the ledger is doubly load-bearing: it's how you audit that each recurring-character meeting plants without leaking. Mirror the 'after final cut' pass — re-check the ledger against actual in-game copy after integration, not just against drafts.
- **Script-coordinator gate: one owner of 'current', diff-driven distribution** — One person is the authority on which draft is current. They proofread, enforce house format, act as 'the first line of defense' on continuity, diff each draft against the last, and alert exactly the departments whose material changed — nobody re-reads the whole script to find out what moved.
  *For glassvow*: The zh source is the single canonical copy set; every change lands as a diff whose entry names the affected string IDs. The en rewriter consumes only marked deltas and never re-reads the corpus — that delta feed IS the two-person zh/en sync mechanism. The AI also runs the coordinator's mechanical pass on every change: name/term consistency grep, format lint, ledger cross-check.
- **Locked pages + colored revisions after lock** — Once the white production draft is distributed, pages lock. Every subsequent change ships as dated, color-coded, revision-marked pages in WGA-standard order (blue, pink, yellow, green, goldenrod...), and only the changed pages are reissued. The title page logs the full revision history; unmarked pages are guaranteed untouched.
  *For glassvow*: Lock zh copy at each build milestone. Post-lock edits carry a revision tag (a rev field per string plus a changelog — the color equivalent), so en rewrite, VO, and UI treat untagged strings as guaranteed-unchanged. This is the cheap insurance against silent zh/en drift, and it makes 'what changed since the last en pass' a query instead of an archaeology dig.
- **Table read as the pre-production quality gate** — The room's Table Draft is read aloud by the actual cast before shooting; hearing it performed exposes dialogue and pacing failures invisible on the page ('the energy will leave the room'); the writers rewrite after the read, before production makes changes expensive. Sitcoms run this gate every episode.
  *For glassvow*: Systemic copy's table read is a scripted full-run playthrough (or a text-flow harness that fires run-start line, interstitials, a meeting-chain scene, and a win/loss beat in real sequence and real pacing) — the human reads the copy in context, not in a spreadsheet. Run it per bucket before Godot wiring hardens, and once per milestone across a whole simulated run. A line that reads fine as a row and dies in flow is exactly what this gate exists to catch.
- **Braintrust note discipline: problems, not prescriptions; no authority, but must-address** — Pixar's Braintrust reviews work-in-progress with candor but no power: notes identify what isn't working (the director 'may not see the problem because much of what he thinks is visible on-screen is only visible in his own head'), never dictate fixes, and the director owns choosing a solution — but may not ignore the problem. Catmull normalizes bad early drafts: 'early on, all of our movies suck.'
  *For glassvow*: Encode as the review loop with the human (and any second-model reviewer per your James-review-delegation setup): reviews file problems ('the reveal telegraphs in meeting 3'), the AI writer owns the fix, and every filed problem needs an explicit resolution or rebuttal — no silent drops. Schedule two braintrust passes per bucket (beat stage, integrated stage) and budget for draft 1 to suck; a pipeline that expects first-draft quality from either language will ship its problems.
- **Keep six stories in flight: pipeline the buckets** — Grillo-Marxuach: at any moment a season has a story on the board, one in outline, one in script, one in prep, one shooting, one in post — the room feeds a conveyor, never one episode at a time end-to-end.
  *For glassvow*: Stagger buckets through fixed stages: while meeting-chain A is integrated and in playtest, B is in en rewrite, C in zh draft, D on the board. A one-line stage tag per bucket in the tracking issue is the entire kanban — it keeps the human reviewer fed with review-ready material instead of alternating between idle and swamped.
- **Showrunner vision doc: draw the boundaries of the sandbox** — The showrunner must articulate 'a strong, clear vision ... with precision, detail, consistency, and integrity' and preach it relentlessly so every writer and department executes on-voice without per-line supervision; the bible's tone section is its written form.
  *For glassvow*: Write a one-page voice charter for 'Hades at lite scale': how each recurring character talks to a player mid-doomed-run, what the game jokes about and what it never does, the zh register and the en register (they will differ; say how). Both languages get reviewed against the charter, and the human acts as showrunner — owns the charter and final notes, doesn't write lines. This is what lets an AI drafter produce 100K chars that read as one author.

## Game studios — narrative design pipelines and tooling

### References

- [The Game Narrative Toolbox (2nd Edition)](https://www.routledge.com/The-Game-Narrative-Toolbox/Heussner-Finley-BrandesHepler-Lemay/p/book/9781032438962) — Tobias Heussner, Toiya Kristen Finley, Jennifer Brandes Hepler, Ann Lemay, 2024 (book)
  Four working narrative designers walk the full production lifecycle; the chapter list (confirmed via the publisher/O'Reilly listings) is: What Is Narrative Design? / The Concept / Worldbuilding / Characters / Story / Implementation and Production / Dialogue / Cutscenes and Cinematics / Troubleshooting. Every chapter's exercises have the reader produce the actual document set — pitch/concept docs, worldbuilding and character documentation, story outlines, dialogue samples — i.e., the book codifies the story-bible/character-doc/narrative-design-doc stack as deliverables tied to production stages. The Routledge/Taylor & Francis product pages 403'd on direct fetch, so the TOC is confirmed from search-surfaced listings, not the page body.
- [Game Narrative Toolbox (review)](https://emshort.blog/2017/11/09/game-narrative-toolbox/) — Emily Short, 2017 (article)
  Independent review confirming what the book actually teaches: template-based character and world documentation, dialogue-tree and quest design, and — notably for pipelines — mundane production discipline like choosing a maximum line length for UI fit and maintaining localization comments on lines. Short's criticism is that the book under-covers mechanics-narrative integration ('story as inlay glued onto the game'), which is exactly the gap a systemic roguelite has to fill itself.
- [Narrative design myth-busting: It's not 'just writing'](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/narrative-design-myth-buster-1-it-s-not-just-writing) — Matthew Weise, 2019 (article)
  Defines the role split: narrative design is a design discipline (like level or systems design) whose toolbox is 'helping create and/or leverage existing mechanics, systems, levels, art, UI, and sound to achieve a desired dramatic experience,' while game writing produces the written content itself — dialogue, descriptions, docs. Argues the confusion persists because games historically treated narrative as a layer separate from gameplay.
- [Game Writing SIG (official page)](https://igda.org/sigs/game-writing/) — IGDA Game Writing SIG, 2026 (article)
  The SIG defines its members' work as 'write dialogue scripts, construct overarching narrative structure, produce documentation and articles' and explicitly spans both writers and narrative designers as distinct roles. The page itself lists only game-writing.com, a Discord, and a mailing list as resources — the SIG's literature lives in its books, not on this page.
- [Game Writing: Narrative Skills for Videogames (2nd Edition)](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/book-excerpt-and-review---game-writing-narrative-skills-for-videogames) — Chris Bateman (ed.), IGDA Game Writing SIG contributors, 2021 (book)
  The first and most popular book by the IGDA Game Writing SIG; Bateman founded the SIG and won an IGDA MVP award for it. Chapters by SIG members cover nonlinear narrative, dialogue, comedy writing, and character craft, with exercises — it is the SIG's codification of game-writing (as opposed to narrative-design) skills. Located via the Game Developer excerpt/review and multiple bookseller listings; I did not fetch the book text itself.
- [Building a basic story bible for your game](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/building-a-basic-story-bible-for-your-game) — Anna Megill (lead writer, Ubisoft Massive), 2019 (article)
  A working AAA lead writer's story-bible template: executive summary (2-3 paragraphs), game tentpoles/themes, worldbuilding (key objects, major events, locations), gameplay systems overview, marketing-relevant material, a 'storytelling methods' section defining exactly how players receive narrative, and reference material for tone. Stresses the bible is a living document that must accommodate change, and that bibles deliberately contain content players never see.
- [Ink: The Narrative Scripting Language Behind '80 Days' and 'Sorcery!' (GDC 2016)](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023221/Ink-The-Narrative-Scripting-Language) — Joseph Humfrey (inkle), 2016 (gdc-talk)
  Session on inkle's in-house scripting language: how a text-first markup language drives game content, facilitates heavy branching, and 'approaches procedural narrative in some scenes.' Announced ink's open-source release with a Unity runtime API, aimed at bringing the writer-friendly workflow to non-text games.
- [ink — narrative scripting language (official site)](https://www.inklestudios.com/ink/) — inkle Ltd (Jon Ingold, Joseph Humfrey), 2026 (tool)
  Positions ink as middleware: 'a narrative engine designed to slot into a game engine,' with 'markup, not programming — text comes first, code and logic are inserted within.' Millions of words of branching narrative shipped across 80 Days, Sorcery!, Heaven's Vault; MIT-licensed with an official Unity plugin (auto-recompile, in-editor preview) and The Chinese Room's Inkpot for Unreal.
- [Ink Localisation Tool (Ink-Localiser)](https://github.com/wildwinter/Ink-Localiser) — Ian Thomas (wildwinter), 2023 (tool)
  Documents the production fix for ink's biggest pipeline gap — no native line IDs: a utility scans ink files, assigns each line a stable ID written back as an ink tag (e.g. #id:dlg_vault_011), and exports strings to CSV/JSON. The prescribed runtime pattern is 'use Ink for logic, not for content': the engine reads the ID tag and looks the string up in the localization table, which also keys VO and other assets. Companion write-up: https://wildwinter.medium.com/ink-localisation-tool-7e321f834794.
- [Yarn Spinner docs — Localisations and Assets](https://docs.yarnspinner.dev/yarn-spinner-for-unity/assets-and-localization) — Yarn Spinner team (Secret Lab), 2026 (tool)
  Yarn Spinner's canonical loc pipeline: declare a base language, auto-add unique line-ID tags to every line ('Add Line Tags to Scripts' rewrites the source files), then 'Export Strings and Metadata as CSV' — export is refused unless every line has an ID. Translators may modify only the language and text columns; touching the id column breaks the link back to the source line. Line metadata (file, node, lineNumber, tags) exports alongside for translator context.
- [articy:draft X — feature list and exports](https://www.articy.com/en/articydraft/feature-list/) — Articy Software, 2026 (tool)
  The AAA-leaning narrative database: visual branching flow with nesting (scenes contain dialogues), entities/variables/conditions as first-class objects, and per-object localization — languages added to the project, all text exported with localization IDs in a re-importable format, plus a dedicated localization view for text and VO. Ready-made Unity/Unreal importers bring dialogues, entities, and variables in 'cleanly structured' with flow traversal and branching-condition support (exports overview: https://www.articy.com/en/adx_basics_exports/).
- [Arcweave — features and localization workflow](https://arcweave.com/features) — Arcweave team, 2026 (tool)
  Collaborative web-based branching tool: node-based editor, reusable world assets (characters, locations, items), variables/conditions via its Arcscript logic language, and official Unity, Unreal, and Godot plugins plus JSON/CSV/XLSX export and a web API. Its localization post (https://blog.arcweave.com/interactive-stories-localization) documents spreadsheet round-tripping with guided reimport, scripted expressions locked non-translatable by default with per-segment opt-in, and per-string translation statuses (Untranslated/In Progress/For Review/Final) that can filter the JSON export so only Final strings ship.

### Transferable practices

- **Split narrative design from game writing as two passes, not two people** — The industry treats these as distinct disciplines (Weise; IGDA SIG; Game Narrative Toolbox ch.1): narrative design decides how the story reaches the player — which systems, triggers, and structures deliver it — while game writing produces the lines. On teams too small for two roles, they are still two documents and two review lenses.
  *For glassvow*: For glassvow, keep a narrative-systems spec (when run-start lines fire, interstitial cadence, meeting-unlock conditions, win/loss beat triggers, spoiler gating for the twist) as a separate artifact from the copy itself. The AI writer drafts lines against that spec; the human reviewer reviews firing logic and prose as separate concerns. Most twist leaks and pacing bugs are design errors, not writing errors, and they're invisible if the two are reviewed together.
- **One living story bible with a 'storytelling methods' section** — Megill's template and the Toolbox's preproduction chapters converge on the same stack: executive summary, tentpoles/themes, world facts, character profiles, and — the underrated section — an explicit list of the methods by which players receive narrative. The bible is a living document containing more than ships, and it exists so every discipline stays consistent without asking the writer.
  *For glassvow*: The glassvow-story skill/foreshadow ledger already is this pattern; the addition worth stealing is the storytelling-methods inventory: enumerate every systemic channel (run-start line, interstitial, win beat, loss beat, each of the five recurring characters, scripted scenes) and record per channel what it is allowed to reveal and at what spoiler tier. For a late-reveal story this table is the twist's firewall — any line can be checked against its channel's ceiling mechanically.
- **Salience-based storylet pools instead of branching trees** — Hades' 22,000 lines are not a tree: the game monitors run conditions (deaths, encounters, thresholds), selects the most applicable pre-authored event from a pool via eligibility + weighting + no-repeat flags, and privileges lines that prove the game noticed the player. Emily Short's 'Beyond Branching' is the formal write-up of this structure (salience-based narrative).
  *For glassvow*: This is the correct data model for ~100K chars of systemic copy: every line is a record with eligibility conditions (run count, last boss, deaths-to-X, character-met-N-times, twist-stage), a priority, and once-only/cooldown flags; a single selector picks the highest-priority eligible line per slot. The twist becomes a high-priority gated chain threaded through evergreen filler. Spend the budget on condition-specific reactivity ('died to the same boss twice') over generic volume — Kasavin's data point is that being noticed is what players remember.
- **Stable line IDs at authoring time; engine uses logic, table supplies text** — Yarn Spinner refuses to export until every line has a unique ID and forbids translators from touching the id column; ink teams retrofit the same via Ink-Localiser (#id: tags, 'use Ink for logic, not content'); articy exports everything with localization IDs for round-trip. In all three, the runtime resolves an ID against a string table — text is data, never code.
  *For glassvow*: Assign every line a stable ID the moment it is drafted (e.g. runstart.act2.death_streak.03) and keep zh source and en rewrite as columns keyed by that ID — Godot's built-in CSV/PO translation system does the lookup natively, so no tooling needs building. The AI writer edits the table, never scene files; IDs never change once assigned, so zh/en stay aligned, review diffs are line-scoped, and the selector code is untouched by copy revisions.
- **Never concatenate; full sentences with named placeholders** — The standing loc rule (Unreal Garden, W3C): sentences assembled from fragments break in any language with different word order — the fix is whole-sentence strings with named, reorderable placeholders ('{GivenNames} {FamilyName}' → '{FamilyName} {GivenName}'), and pseudo-loc passes to catch hardcoded text.
  *For glassvow*: zh and en differ in word order constantly, so this bites a two-language pipeline immediately: every systemic line that mentions a boss, relic, or run stat must be a complete sentence per language with named placeholders ({boss}, {deaths}) — Godot's String.format supports this. Ban fragment-joining in GDScript for player-facing text; a grep-based check in check_scripts-style CI is cheap and catches it forever.
- **Character-limit columns and expansion budgeting per string class** — W3C/IBM data: the shorter the source string, the more translation expands it (200-300% under 10 chars). Production loc kits annotate each string with its limit; the Game Narrative Toolbox teaches choosing a maximum line length for UI fit as a writing-time constraint, not a QA-time discovery.
  *For glassvow*: Chinese is denser than English, so the en rewrite will systematically overflow anything fitted to zh — the premium-mobile UI makes this worse (small fixed layouts). Give each string class a max-length budget (interstitial line, toast, card text, meeting dialogue) as a column in the copy table, sized for the en column, and enforce it with a trivial CI check over the CSV. Cheaper than reflowing UI after the rewrite lands.
- **Loc kit as writer kit: glossary, voice guides, per-string context** — Keywords' loc-kit standard: style guide (tone/register), glossary of every proper noun, character profiles, and context for every string (where it appears, who speaks it) — because a bare 'Ready?' is untranslatable. Vendors run a familiarization phase before touching a line.
  *For glassvow*: The en rewrite is a localization, and the AI writer is the vendor — so build the same kit once and both languages benefit: a locked glossary of proper-noun renderings (zh term → canonical en term), a voice sheet per recurring character (register, verbal tics, what they never say), and context + speaker + spoiler-tier columns on every row of the copy table. This is also exactly the context an AI writer needs to stay in voice across sessions, and the human reviewer's checklist falls out of it for free.
- **Screenplay format only for scripted scenes; database for everything systemic** — Studios draft cinematics and scenes in Final Draft-style screenplay format (readable, actor/VO-standard), then decompose them into spreadsheet line records — IDs, filename codes, speaker, cue type, metadata with enum validation — which become the implementable source of truth exported as TSV/JSON (Crawford; Toolbox's Implementation chapter).
  *For glassvow*: Draft the five recurring-character meeting chains and the few scripted scenes as short screenplays first — that format is what the human reviewer can actually judge for drama and twist pacing — then compile them into the string table with IDs. The systemic 90% (run-start lines, interstitials, win/loss beats) skips the screenplay stage and lives natively as table rows. Review prose in the script, review delivery in the table.
- **Tool choice by team scale — and at this scale, plain data wins** — The tooling tiers are consistent across sources: articy:draft is a multi-user narrative database for large teams; ink is for prose-heavy hyper-branching (millions of words at inkle); Yarn Spinner for line-based dialogue with the strictest loc discipline; Arcweave for collaborative web editing with a Godot plugin; Twine for prototyping structure visually before committing.
  *For glassvow*: Glassvow's copy is systemic, not branching, and the team is two — so the shipping format should be the CSV/JSON storylet table plus a small GDScript selector, not a licensed middleware layer (adopting articy here would be pure overhead; even ink's flow model solves a branching problem this game doesn't have). The one borrow worth making: a throwaway Twine or Arcweave map of the five character arcs and twist gates, purely to let the reviewer see the reveal structure before any prose exists.
- **Tune the firing model with placeholder copy before final prose** — Supergiant used Early Access to validate that the narrative cadence survived the roguelike loop — the selection system, weights, and death-as-story-beat framing were iterated live, with the script's implementation decoupled from its content (GDC 2021 talk).
  *For glassvow*: Because IDs decouple selection from text, glassvow can playtest meeting cadence, interstitial frequency, and twist-gate pacing with placeholder lines ('[Yune reacts to third loss]') before the AI writer drafts a word — the expensive human-review budget then goes to prose that already sits at a validated moment. Mistimed reveals get fixed by editing conditions in the table, never by rewriting copy.

### Transferable practices

- **Make the loop the story's delivery mechanism (death/loss is the richest beat)** — Hades' founding question was a roguelike with narrative continuity: immortal characters canonically remember every run, bosses keep a tally of wins and losses, and every death returns you to a hub where the cast reacts to exactly how you died. The explicit goal was that 'the moment of death isn't about rage-quitting' — failure is when the story pays out, so players want to lose forward.
  *For glassvow*: Budget the ~100K chars so loss beats and post-run hub copy are the deepest pools, not win beats — players will see losses far more often. Give the five recurring characters memory flags (met_count, last_outcome, tally) so meeting N reads differently from meeting 1. Pick a fictional premise in which repetition is canon; for a twist story this is doubly useful, because the reason the loop repeats can itself be the late reveal.
- **One shared line-picker: eligibility conditions, then priority, then weighted random, with cooldowns and once-only flags** — Every Hades conversation is data in Lua files (NPCData, LootData, EnemyData, EncounterData) with game-state requirements. The picker filters the bucket by conditions; essential story beats override everything; among eligible entries, priority-flagged ones are chosen first (random among equal priority); one conversation per NPC per encounter; gods speak only on first meeting per run; repeats are avoided until unused options are exhausted.
  *For glassvow*: Build exactly one Godot system for all systemic copy (run-start, interstitials, win/loss, meetings): each line = {id, speaker, slot, conditions[], priority, once_only, cooldown_runs, weight, zh, en}. Three tiers — evergreen filler, condition-triggered flavor, essential beats that preempt. This is a few hundred lines of GDScript plus a data file; the sophistication lives in the authored conditions, not the engine. Keep selection seeded/deterministic so parity fixtures and tests can replay it.
- **Spend words on narrow, hyper-specific reactions — 'the game is paying attention'** — Reactivity was a stated narrative-design goal. Supergiant's own stats show the skew: Hypnos alone has 75 unique responses to distinct causes of death; Zagreus holds 8,500 of 21,000 lines (reactions to boons, weapons, events) while whole characters live on ~120 lines. A handful of lines that name exactly what you just did buys disproportionate perceived depth.
  *For glassvow*: At 100K chars you cannot out-write repetition, so out-aim it: reserve maybe 15-20% of the budget for narrow-condition lines (died to a specific boss twice running, won a run without using a signature card, first time drafting a degenerate combo). One character who comments on causes of death/defeat, Hypnos-style, is the cheapest 'this game sees me' machine a deckbuilder can have — the run already produces the stats.
- **Engineer when repetition is felt, and measure it in loops, not lines** — Kasavin's stated bar mid-EA: 'before any of it repeats, you would have to have looped through the game like 20 or 30 times.' Hades hits that with brute volume (300K words) plus exhaustion-aware selection that prefers unplayed lines, and per-encounter cooldowns so no character ever babbles twice in one visit.
  *For glassvow*: Set an explicit loop budget per slot (e.g. run-start lines must survive 15 runs before a repeat) and make it a test: simulate N runs headless and assert no distinctive line repeats inside the window. At lite scale the trick is asymmetric repeatability — mark distinctive/joke lines once_only (a repeated zinger breaks the spell), keep a small pool of deliberately bland idle lines that can repeat safely, and let characters fall silent rather than repeat something memorable.
- **Pilot-episode structure: ship story in slices, test characters live, write the ending last** — EA launch was treated 'like a pilot episode'; every major update (monthly/bi-monthly) added a character plus systems plus a batch of story — the Long Winter update alone shipped Demeter, 1,000+ voice lines and 'many narrative events.' New characters were validated instantly ('we find out right away if people like this character'); Dusa was promoted from comic relief because EA players loved her; the endings were deliberately written last, 'after everything else, knowing what we want to do with it,' and reserved for 1.0.
  *For glassvow*: For a premium mobile title the analog is soft-launch/TestFlight waves: ship the systemic copy and the five character arcs early, hold the twist and finale out of every test build (Supergiant proves withholding the ending doesn't hurt EA reception — it protects it). Write the reveal and ending last, once the beloved-character data is in. A two-person team's feedback channel is telemetry (dialogue skip rate, tap-through speed per character) plus a small Discord/beta group — pick per-character engagement as the metric before the first wave.
- **Continuity by state flags and premise, not by conversation ordering** — Hades never tracks 'which line played before' as narrative truth; lines key off durable state (deaths, tallies, gifts given, subplot flags), and the immortal-cast premise absorbs any ordering the picker produces. Kasavin: 'The scripting was mostly simple, except when it wasn't' — the hard cases (Achilles' subplot) were hard precisely because many pathways led into one subplot, i.e. where ordering did matter.
  *For glassvow*: Make every line's meaning order-tolerant: condition on flags (boss_defeated, character_affinity, runs_cleared), never on adjacency to another line, so the picker can shuffle freely without contradiction. For the twist this is load-bearing: maintain a knowledge ledger (the project's foreshadow-ledger already is one) mapping each flag to what the player provably knows, and have the human reviewer audit each line's condition set for post-twist knowledge leaking into pre-reveal states. Expect and budget extra scripting time for the one or two subplots with multiple entry paths.
- **The writer authors directly in the game's data; production stays closet-scale** — One narrative designer (Kasavin) wrote the entire 300K-word script into Supergiant's own Lua data format — deliberately unobfuscated, mostly simple scripting — so the script and its trigger conditions never lived in a separate document that could drift. Audio was the same shape: Korb recorded Bastion's 3,000 and then Hades' 22,000 lines in an apartment-closet studio, iterating takes 'until it was just exactly what we were looking for,' with audio involved from day one.
  *For glassvow*: Author lines directly in the shipped resource format (one JSON/tres per character or slot), with zh source, en rewrite, and conditions in the same record — a bilingual pair that shares one condition set can never desync, and the AI writer's output is immediately runnable in-game rather than transcribed from a doc. The human reviewer reviews the data file diff, not a screenplay. This is the Hades pipeline shrunk to its actual essence: zero tooling beyond the data format plus instant in-game verification.
- **Pyre's lesson: write the loss variants nobody may see, and iterate on reading length early** — Pyre had no game-over — the story continued through every defeat — which forced Kasavin to write variant versions of events 'that a given player will never see,' and he reports that 'how much reading should there be took a lot of iteration to figure out.' That variant-writing muscle and length calibration is what made Hades' 21,000-line reactive script feel tractable rather than terrifying.
  *For glassvow*: Treat win/loss variants as one beat, authored together — a beat without a loss variant is unfinished, since roguelite players lose most runs and your twist must land for players who never got good. On mobile, reading length is the equivalent iteration: fix a hard per-line character cap (zh and en will differ — cap both), put it in the schema, and playtest text-length tolerance in the first soft-launch wave rather than after the full 100K chars are written.

### Transferable practices

- **Most-specific-rule-wins with a fallback ladder** — Valve's Response System (Ruskin, GDC 2012): every line is a rule with a list of criteria over a fact dictionary (who, what event, health, map, history). On a speech event, all matching rules are scored by how many criteria they carry; the most specific one fires, and generic low-criteria rules act as guaranteed fallbacks, so the box never goes silent and writers add specificity incrementally.
  *For glassvow*: Make every run-start line, interstitial, and win/loss beat a row in one flat table: {id, criteria over run state + story flags, priority, zh, en}. Selection = filter eligible, sort by criteria count, tie-break randomly. Author each slot in tiers: 1 generic always-true line, a few mid-specificity lines, a handful of sharp ones (died-to-boss-X-twice, first-ever-win). ~100K chars is roughly 1.5–3K short lines — a table, not a graph; in Godot this is one Resource/CSV and a 30-line picker, no dialogue-tree middleware.
- **Persistent memory: once-only flags, cooldowns, and no-repeat bookkeeping** — The Response System remembers facts and marks responses to avoid immediate repeats (re-speak delays, first-time-only responses); Hades additionally weights candidate events and conditions them on what the player just did, so lines feel like the game 'is paying attention' (Kasavin).
  *For glassvow*: Three columns in the same table: once (story beats, twist material — plays exactly once, flag persists in the save), cooldown_runs (flavor lines can't repeat within N runs), weight. This is the single cheapest 'Hades feel' purchase: a loss beat that names the boss that killed you, once, beats fifty generic loss lines. The seen-flags set must live in the save file and be versioned like any other save data.
- **Storylet = preconditions + content + effects, as the only content unit** — Kreminski & Wardrip-Fruin (ICIDS 2018) show every storylet system reduces to discrete units with preconditions, content, and effects, differing only in selection architecture and repeatability; Emily Short's posts argue this decoupling from a fixed graph is what makes large pools maintainable.
  *For glassvow*: Model the five recurring-character meetings as five small storylet decks: each meeting has preconditions (affinity level, story flags, run count), plays its scene, and sets flags/raises affinity as effects. The scripted scenes are just storylets whose preconditions are strict enough that they can only fire once, in order. Because each unit is small and self-contained, the zh→en rewrite and human review happen per-storylet — reviewable diffs instead of a monolithic script.
- **Progress qualities with named structural patterns, aligned to game resources** — Failbetter's 'New Narrative Structures' names reusable QBN shapes — Midnight Staircase (one progress quality, choose your payoff floor), Carousel (a timing quality cycles content and resets), Grandfather Clock (fast quality ticks a slow main arc). Kennedy's later 'resource narrative' correction: qualities must interact with the game's real resources and force trade-offs, or the system decays into isolated flags.
  *For glassvow*: Per-character affinity is a Grandfather Clock: runs generate meetings (minute hand) that tick the character's arc (hour hand). Use the Carousel for repeatable flavor meetings between arc beats so characters never run dry. Per Kennedy, gate story on things the deckbuilder already tracks (wins, deck archetype, boss kills, currency spent) rather than inventing story-only counters — the twist lands harder when its prerequisites are things the player did, not a hidden odometer.
- **A thin drama manager above the line-picker; salience alone must not own the twist** — Façade (Mateas & Stern, AIIDE 2005) sequences beats from a pool via a drama manager enforcing an Aristotelian tension arc — local reactivity inside a beat, global ordering across beats. L4D's AI Director does the same for pacing: build-up, peak, relax, per run.
  *For glassvow*: Two layers: (1) a scheduler that, at each narrative slot, first asks 'is a story beat due?' (per-run budget: at most one major beat per run, reveals in ledger order, minimum spacing between twist advances); (2) only if no beat is due does the salience picker choose from the reactive/flavor pool. Free-order salience is for flavor; the twist chain is waypointed. This is the whole drama manager — a priority queue and three constants, not an architecture.
- **Defensive logic for order-independence (foreshadow discipline)** — Ingold's 'Narrative Sorcery' (GDC 2017): in open-order prose games, script defensively — every line that references an earlier event checks the flag proving the player saw it, and a neutral variant exists for when they didn't; the story must read coherently along any path.
  *For glassvow*: With a late-reveal story delivered through shuffled run-lines, this is the load-bearing rule: any line that would read differently post-twist gets two variants gated on the reveal flag; any callback checks the seen-flag of what it calls back to. Encode it as data, not vigilance: a lint over the line table that flags rows referencing a flag no eligible-earlier row can set — the same shape as this repo's anchor-checker gates, and it protects both language versions at once.
- **Every bark does two jobs; accept/reject/deflect keeps lines short** — Barks craft (Narrative Dept, Beaulieu; Redding's Far Cry 2 example of one bark conveying three facts): a systemic line carries its surface function plus implied character/relationship/mood. Ingold's 'Sparkling Dialogue': characters accept, reject, or deflect rather than answer plainly, and every line shifts the power balance.
  *For glassvow*: Spec each of the 5 characters with a one-line 'deflection style' (how they dodge, what they never say) and require every run-start/interstitial line to carry one function payload (react to run state) plus one character-or-foreshadow payload. Short, oblique lines also survive the zh→en rewrite far better than ornate prose — the double-duty rule is the review checklist item for both writers.
- **Annotate what each line asserts; generate wide, polish by hand** — Ryan et al. (ICIDS 2014) annotate lines with what they express about game state and what may precede/follow them, then recombine a small human-written base into many exchanges; machine output rated below human but expressed game state twice as strongly, with the intended workflow being computer-generates, human-polishes.
  *For glassvow*: This paper describes your exact pipeline (AI writer drafts variant pools per slot, human reviewer polishes). The transferable move is the annotation:每行 carries machine-readable metadata of what it asserts (state expressed, flags referenced, tone). That one schema serves triple duty — eligibility criteria for the picker, the defensive-logic lint input, and the zh/en parity checklist (both languages must assert the same facts even when the prose diverges).
- **Writer-comfortable tooling: copy lives in data, never in code** — Ruskin's talk closes on it: the rule-database approach 'empowers your writers' only if the authoring surface is comfortable — Valve's writers edit plain-text rule scripts, not engine code; Hades' 22K lines were likewise implementable because the script format was optimized for the production pipeline.
  *For glassvow*: Keep all ~100K chars in one spreadsheet-shaped source of truth (id, criteria, priority, once/cooldown, zh, en, asserts, sets) compiled to a Godot Resource at import. The human reviewer reviews CSV diffs, the parity between zh and en is a column-pair check, and no narrative change ever touches GDScript — which also keeps the copy out of the parity-fixture blast radius.

### Transferable practices

- **Ending first, locked before volume writing** — Coben: 'I know the ending before I even start writing... I lock that ending into my mental GPS.' Only Murders' room 'had to work backwards and know where we're aiming and who killed and how, then track backwards and twist it.' Shyamalan for serialized work: 'have the ending in mind before starting it, so that in every episode you can write traces of that ending.'
  *For glassvow*: Write and lock the reveal document FIRST — the full twist text in both zh-Hant and English, plus the list of canonical facts it depends on — before drafting any of the ~100K chars of systemic copy. Every copy-slot brief (run-start line, interstitial, meeting, win/loss beat) then cites which reveal-fact it serves or explicitly serves none. The reveal doc becomes the root artifact of the story bible; changing it after volume writing triggers a full ledger re-audit, so treat it like a save-format version bump.
- **A codified, testable fair-play contract** — Knox and Van Dine wrote the contract as enforceable rules (culprit introduced early, no clue withheld from the reader, no last-minute imported facts). CrimeReads' modern distillation: the clues were on the page before the reveal, the culprit was already inside the story, and the solution changes the meaning of earlier details instead of adding a missing fact.
  *For glassvow*: Write a project-specific decalogue into the glassvow-story skill (5-8 rules, e.g. 'the truth is fully derivable from copy every player is guaranteed to see', 'the twist's key figure appears on-screen before meeting 3 of 5', 'the reveal may only reinterpret shipped lines, never introduce a new fact'). The human reviewer runs each reveal beat against it like a lint gate; a rule violation is a blocker, not a taste note. Two-person pipelines especially need this — the contract substitutes for the writers' room argument.
- **Double-reading audit: every line true under both readings, in both languages** — The Sixth Sense was retrofitted across ~five drafts so every Malcolm scene plays honestly whether or not you know he's dead, with hard world-rules (red = contamination by the dead) enforced scene-by-scene and the crew 'very rigorous about maintaining the laws and the rules of that world' explicitly for second-viewing scrutiny. Memento's Nolan withheld exactly what the protagonist lacked, no more.
  *For glassvow*: Every foreshadowing line in the ledger carries two glosses: surface meaning (pre-reveal) and true meaning (post-reveal). The reviewer does two full passes of the copy pool — one naive, one post-reveal — and does it in BOTH zh and en, because double meanings routinely die in translation: an ambiguity that carries the twist in Traditional Chinese (topic-drop, unmarked subjects, homophones) must be re-engineered in English, not translated. Budget the reviewer's single naive read carefully — naivety is a nonrenewable resource; spend it on the near-final draft.
- **Illusion of fair play: control salience, not presence** — Rian Johnson: the whodunnit's magic trick is making clues register without being understood — noticed on first pass so the denouement feels like connecting recognized dots — while written misdirection (not omission) keeps them from being decoded early. He warns against 'one big buildup to a cheap surprise' and puts 'a different engine in the car' plus Hitchcock-style mid-story partial reveals.
  *For glassvow*: Every twist-critical plant gets a WRITTEN in-fiction decoy explanation (the character has a plausible mundane reason for the odd line), so misdirection is authored, not hoped for. And don't hold everything for the finale of a 20+ hour roguelite: schedule mid-arc partial reveals — e.g. meeting 3 of 5 for each recurring character flips one lesser secret — so the story has engines between the first run and the true ending, exactly where Christie-style plots 'flag three-quarters through'.
- **Structure as the trick: make the delivery mechanism the concealment mechanism** — Memento's backward structure IS the protagonist's condition ('withhold from the audience what's withheld from the protagonist'); The Prestige's screenplay is literally shaped as Pledge/Turn/Prestige, performing the misdirection it depicts. Hades aligns premise with loop: an immortal hero makes death diegetic, so the roguelite reset is story, not interruption.
  *For glassvow*: Don't fight systemic delivery — make the run loop the twist's substrate. The strongest move for a Hades-scale roguelite: the repetition/reset/death mechanic is itself the thing the late reveal reinterprets (why the runs repeat, why characters remember, what the 'world journey' actually is). Then fragmented, out-of-order copy stops being a liability: the player's confusion is the protagonist's, which is Nolan's empathy argument for withholding.
- **Foreshadow ledger, re-audited on every draft** — Dark's Jantje Friese: charts plus Post-Its, and after every draft 'you kind of have to look at everything again: Do I need to plant this differently? And where is this leading now?... map it out again, write new Post-Its' — plus color-coding reserved per timeline. Severance keeps a single canonical lore document ('Dan has it all written out... not retrofitting') that pre-answers every open mystery.
  *For glassvow*: Keep a version-controlled ledger file (this repo already runs check_anchors.py-style gates — add a check_foreshadow analog): each plant row has an ID, the copy-slot key(s) it lives in, zh and en line refs, its payoff ID, and a status (planted / paid / orphaned). A script fails the gate on payoffs with zero surviving plants and on plants whose payoff was cut. Any edit to either language's line re-opens the row — Dark's lesson is that drift happens per-draft, so the audit must be per-draft, and with an AI writer generating volume, mechanical enforcement replaces the room's memory.
- **Information-control matrix: who knows what, when — gated on counters, not chronology** — Mystery rooms fix the solution first, then track knowledge states backward (Only Murders: 'creating the true crime, then piecing it together'); Dark charted every character across timelines; Severance's doc fixes what's true before deciding when anyone learns it. Hades gates lines on observable state (meeting counts, deaths, current health) rather than scene order.
  *For glassvow*: Build the matrix as data: rows = canonical facts from the reveal doc; columns = the five recurring characters + the player; cells = one of {knows, suspects, believes-false, ignorant}, each keyed to an observable progression counter (meeting N, wins, deaths, chapter flags) — never to 'after scene X', because systemic delivery guarantees no scene order. Every line in the 100K pool declares its required knowledge state; the selection system (and a lint pass) refuses a line whose speaker's cell doesn't permit it. This is the single highest-leverage artifact for a two-person pipeline: it converts 'did we leak this early?' from a memory question into a query.
- **Redundant, order-independent planting with an optional deep layer** — Scriptnotes' craft: setups must take root without announcing themselves, which in practice means multiple disguised touches. The Afterparty nested ciphers and anagrams for the Reddit-forensics audience — expensive ('a real pain in the butt') but never load-bearing; the solvable spine sat in plain sight across every character's POV.
  *For glassvow*: In a run-based game any single line can go unseen, so a twist-critical plant must exist in 3+ independent copy slots across different delivery channels (a run-start line, a loss beat, a meeting) with different surface wording — redundancy in facts, variety in phrasing, so no player sees the seams and every player sees the plant. Separately, seed a thin optional deep layer (item flavor text, environment copy) for the community-wiki audience — it drives social rewatch value, but the fair-play contract forbids the reveal depending on it.
- **Retro-planting as a scheduled rewrite pass, not an aspiration** — Shyamalan found the twist at draft five and spent roughly five MORE full drafts retrofitting every scene for the double reading, rewriting big-to-small (acts, scenes, then dialogue). The Prestige took the Nolans five years, 18 months just for structure. Coben cuts ten candidate twists for every one that ships.
  *For glassvow*: Put an explicit 'plant pass' milestone in the pipeline after the reveal text is final: the AI writer sweeps all copy against the ledger — adding plants where a payoff is under-planted, sanding plants that read as setups, updating both languages together — and the human reviewer verifies rows, not vibes. Budget real time for it: the craft record says the twist's quality lives in this retrofit, not in the initial drafting, and at 100K chars the sweep is precisely the kind of exhaustive mechanical-plus-judgment work the AI-writer/human-reviewer split is best at.
- **Reactivity engine with simulation-verified plant firing** — Hades checks in-run conditions and serves the best-matching pre-written event from a weighted pool, prioritizing story-relevant lines so beats land despite randomness while the player feels 'the game is paying attention.' Supergiant's guarantee is systemic: important lines outrank flavor when their conditions are met.
  *For glassvow*: Give the copy system a priority tier: twist-critical plants outrank flavor whenever their matrix conditions hold, with per-line cooldowns so they don't cluster. Then close the loop with a headless simulation (the repo already runs deterministic fixture tests): simulate a few hundred typical runs and assert every twist-critical plant fires — in multiple slots — before its payoff can trigger, and that no post-reveal-gated line can fire pre-reveal. A ledger row flips to 'planted' only when the simulation proves it, not when the line is written; that is the mechanical equivalent of Frank Marshall protecting the second viewing.
